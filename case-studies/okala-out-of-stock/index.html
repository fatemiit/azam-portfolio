Okala · Quick-commerce

The item you wanted is gone. Now what?

How suggesting alternatives inside the cart turned an out-of-stock dead end into 337 extra orders a day, while leaving the final choice to the shopper.

The problem

A red label, right before payment

Quick-commerce runs on a simple promise: what you see is what arrives in half an hour. But stock in a dark store moves by the minute. Between the moment someone adds yogurt to their cart and the moment they tap checkout, that yogurt can be gone.

When I looked at the funnel, the gap was hard to ignore. About 8% of cart sessions included at least one out-of-stock item, and those sessions converted much worse than the rest.

Checkout conversion by cart state

Same funnel, same week. The only difference is an unavailable item in the cart.

No unavailable items

70.4%

At least one unavailable item

46.9%

A 23.5-point drop, one step away from payment.

The existing experience didn’t help. Shoppers saw a red label and two bad options: abandon the whole cart, or delete the item and go back to searching from scratch. Either way, we were asking people to do our job for us.

People hadn’t changed their minds about buying. We had just made the next step hard.

Listening

What makes a swap feel acceptable?

Before sketching anything, we wanted to know when a replacement feels like help and when it feels like being short-changed. We ran a survey with 2,081 shoppers and followed it with in-depth interviews.

Brand

Loyalty decides

For dairy, drinks and detergents, people stick to brands they trust. A random substitute feels like a downgrade.

Size

Volume must match

For staples like oil or tissues, the same pack size matters more than the logo on it.

Price

A little more is fine

Shoppers will pay a bit more for a trusted brand. For basics, they expect the same price or less.

Pack

Familiar looks

Recognisable packaging helps people decide in a couple of seconds.

The interviews were even clearer. Every participant asked us not to swap things for them. They wanted to see a few options and choose themselves, and they didn’t want a long list, because a long list is just another search page.

All five interviewees rejected automatic replacement. They wanted to choose.

The design

Rescue the order without leaving the cart

The plan was simple: keep people in the cart, show a short list of good alternatives, and let them swap with one tap. This is the flow we shipped.

Before

The unavailable item jumped to the top of the cart with an “Unavailable” label. The only thing you could do was delete it.

After

A “Similar items” button now opens up to ten in-stock alternatives, matched on sub-category and price. Tap + and the alternative takes the missing item’s place.

The swap flow

Every step happens inside the cart. No new page, and the scroll position stays where it was.

Step 1

Unavailable item is flagged

A clear label, without breaking the layout of the cart.

Option A

Remove all unavailable

For shoppers in a hurry.

Option B

See similar items

For shoppers who still want the item.

Step 2

Swap in place

The alternative takes the exact spot of the missing item.

Step 3

Checkout

The order goes through.

Deciding which alternatives to show was a design problem as much as an algorithm problem. Together with the data team we set four ranking rules, all shaped by what shoppers told us.

Rule 1

Same shelf first

Match the sub-category, then widen to the category.

Rule 2

Closest price

Sort by the smallest price difference.

Rule 3

Actually in stock

Only items with healthy stock, so we don’t send people to another dead end.

Rule 4

Ten at most

A short, curated list instead of the full catalogue.

Small details mattered too. If someone had three of the missing item, we added one of the alternative and let them set the quantity. Guessing wrong either way would have cost trust.

Iterating

The quiet grey button

Our first release used a calm, grey secondary button for “See similar items”. It looked clean. It also stalled at around 6% click-through. Our hypothesis: someone facing an error is looking for a way out, and a low-contrast button simply disappeared next to the warning.

Click-through on “See similar items”

Same placement and copy. Only the visual weight changed.

6.0%

Grey secondary button

8.6%

High-contrast brand button

+33% click-through after the change.

We also planned for the moments when we had nothing good to offer. In about 4.65% of cases no suitable alternative existed, so instead of an empty panel we showed a short hint that helps people remove the item and keep going.

The number that surprised me most: 8.2% of people tapped “Remove all” and 8.6% tapped “See similar items”. Once there was an easy option, about half of the people who hit a dead end saved their order.

Looking back

What I’m taking with me

Lesson

Clarity beats decoration

In error states, contrast and affordance matter more than a minimal look.

Lesson

Design depends on data

The experience is only as good as how fresh the stock data is.

Lesson

Protect the user’s place

Swapping in place, without a jump or refresh, keeps people feeling in control.

Next on the roadmap: pinning unavailable items to the top of the cart, personalising alternatives with purchase history, and bringing the same logic to the pickers who pack orders in the warehouse.
